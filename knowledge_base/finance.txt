A basic startup budget should separate one-time setup costs (equipment, licenses, initial inventory, website/branding) from recurring monthly costs (rent, salaries, utilities, marketing, raw materials). New founders often underestimate recurring costs, which causes cash flow problems even when the business idea itself is sound.

Pricing strategy options include cost-plus pricing (adding a fixed margin on top of production cost), competitive pricing (matching or slightly undercutting competitor prices), and value-based pricing (pricing according to the perceived value to the customer rather than production cost). Service businesses with strong differentiation can often use value-based pricing for higher margins.

Maintaining a cash flow buffer of 3-6 months of operating expenses is a common recommendation for new small businesses, since revenue in the first several months is often unpredictable while fixed costs continue regardless of sales volume.

Sources of small business funding include personal savings (bootstrapping), friends and family loans, bank/MSME loans, government startup schemes, angel investors, and crowdfunding. Bootstrapping keeps full ownership but limits initial scale; external funding accelerates growth but usually requires giving up equity or taking on repayment obligations.

Break-even analysis — calculating how many units must be sold, or how much revenue must be earned, to cover all fixed and variable costs — helps a founder understand the minimum sales volume required before the business becomes profitable, and is a useful number to calculate before committing a large investment.

Basic bookkeeping practices (tracking every expense and sale, separating personal and business finances, reconciling accounts monthly) are essential even for very small businesses, both for tax compliance and for understanding real profitability rather than just cash in the bank.
